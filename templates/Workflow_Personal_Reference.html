The Real Opportunity: What Comes After Prototype

⚠ Watch This

The Handoff to Engineering and Beyond

You're about to do something your organization probably hasn't done before: hand engineering a working prototype with actual user validation. That's powerful, but it creates a new set of challenges.

Three Things You Need to Manage Explicitly

1. Engineering Doesn't Rebuild; They Harden
Your prototype works for one client viewing one dataset. Production needs to handle 1000 clients, edge cases, mobile responsiveness, security, performance, integrations. Your engineers need permission to write production code, not reverse-engineer your prototype code.

Action: Before handing it over, be clear in your brief: "This prototype is a spec. Here's what works conceptually. Now do the production work."
2. Organizational Friction Around "Control"
Your account executives feel they control the narrative with the 105-slide deck. Moving to self-service data access feels like loss of control. Your counter-argument (investment advisor model) is solid, but you need to actively manage this perception.

Action: Frame the prototype conversation with sales leadership as "this elevates your role from data delivery to strategic guidance." That's the actual truth. Don't skip this conversation.
3. Pilot Validation Before Scale
You have a working prototype. The temptation will be to roll it out to all clients once it's productionized. Resist that. You need to validate the concept with a real client first—measure whether they actually prefer it, whether they ask different questions, whether they use it like you expect.

Action: Get sales leadership to identify one pilot client. Use that to gather actual data on whether your hypothesis is right before you bet the whole quarterly review cycle on it.

Where Your Engineering Engagement Matters

You mentioned you haven't handed the prototype to engineering yet. This is strategic. Right now, you should:

  1. Get sales buy-in first. Prototype → SVP of Sales conversation → identify pilot client.
  2. Involve engineering early, but not yet on the build. Show them the prototype. Ask: "What production requirements aren't obvious here?" Performance? Mobile? Integration with existing systems? Data latency? They'll see things you won't.
  3. Let them inform your pilot plan. If they say "this will be hard to scale to 1000 clients," you want to know that before you commit to the pilot, not after.
  4. Only then hand them the spec. By then, it's clear, and they know what they're building.

The Real Question You Should Revisit Quarterly

Where is your actual bottleneck right now?

Is it:

  • Getting stakeholder buy-in on the concept? (Then you need better prototyping or positioning)
  • Getting stakeholders to actually commit resources? (Then it's org dynamics, not AI)
  • Engineering execution speed? (Different problem entirely)
  • Something else?

Don't optimize the phases of your workflow. Optimize around the actual constraint. Right now, your constraint isn't research, planning, or prototyping. It's probably organizational alignment and engineering capacity.

Is there something that takes unexpectedly long that shouldn't?

If you find yourself spending 2 weeks on something that should take 2 days, that's a signal. Right now, you've said no—there's no shortage of work and the process is efficient. Good. Keep that standard. The moment something feels unnecessarily slow, that's where the next optimization lives.

The Gotchas You Need to Actively Avoid

1. Don't conflate "working prototype" with "production ready." Your React dashboard works. Engineering will need to rebuild parts of it for production. Don't get attached to the prototype code. Use it as a spec.
2. Don't let the speed fool you into building things you don't need. The fact that you can prototype something quickly doesn't mean you should. Your filter (research + planning) before prototyping is what keeps this from becoming a collection of half-baked ideas. Don't skip the thinking.
3. Don't assume "we built a prototype" = "we validated the concept." Your 105-slide deck prototype is great, but you haven't run it with a real client yet. You have an artifact and a hypothesis. Validation comes later.
4. Watch for AI hallucinating requirements. Claude is helpful to a fault. It will add features that sound good but weren't in your spec. Review every addition and ask: "Did I actually ask for this?" If not, delete it. Scope creep happens at the prototype stage, and it's cheap to prevent.
5. Don't use multiple AI tools to confirm your thinking—use them to challenge it. You're already doing this correctly. Keep doing it. The moment you start using them to validate instead of poke holes, you've lost the value.
